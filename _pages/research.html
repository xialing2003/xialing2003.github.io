A simple discrete elastic model of slow slip

Ling Xia and Allan M. Rubin · Department of Geosciences, Princeton University · Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, 2026

Slow slip events and tremor have been modeled in two separate ways: elastic/frictional models that reproduce how events migrate, and Brownian models that reproduce their statistics. We build one minimal model that does both, and show analytically how the two descriptions connect.

The model

The fault is a 2D grid of 50 m elements embedded in a 3D elastic medium and driven between two rigid walls 30 km apart. Each element is either locked or slipping at a fixed slow speed Vslip. A locked element starts to slip when its stress reaches its static strength. That strength is τp if the element has not slipped in the current event, and a reduced τ′p = (1 − εD)τp if it has. When an event ends, all strengths return to τp.

Key results

~1 yr

Recurrence interval

Characteristic fault-spanning events recur about once a year, matching analytical fracture-mechanics estimates (minimum ≈ 0.62 yr for εD = 0.6).

~10 km/day

Propagation speed

The main front advances at about 9–15 km/day, close to the analytical estimate and to observed tremor migration.

f−1

Spectral decay

The moment-rate amplitude spectrum falls off as f−1 above a corner near 2×10−5 Hz, as in Brownian models and observations.

M̈₀² ∝ Ṁ₀

Constant scaled energy

Squared moment acceleration is proportional to moment rate, the signature of constant scaled energy.

A linear birth–death process, with rates computed from the elastic kernel and the reduced strength, explains these statistics. It gives the equilibrium moment rate (1.24×1011 N·m/s against 1.23×1011 from the elastic estimate), the corner frequency, and the ratio M̈₀²/Ṁ₀ (1.4×107 against 1.3×107 N·m/s³ simulated).

What the model does not capture

The simulated scaled energy is about six orders of magnitude lower than observed, and the moment rate is steadier than real tremor. We interpret the fixed Vslip of order 1 μm/s as an average over a much wider range of slip speeds in nature. Rapid reversals appear, but at speeds up to about 10 times the main front rather than the observed 25–50 times.
